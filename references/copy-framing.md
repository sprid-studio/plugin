# Finding the frame

A frame is the angle a piece of copy takes on the reader's situation: the pain they
feel, the result they want, the thing they would use otherwise. The same product can
be framed many ways, and the right one depends on who reads it and what they already
know. These methods are questions to ask about this reader. Research the
answers for each app and each page from its own evidence.

## Research the frame before writing

- **Mine their words.** Read what the readers write when nobody is selling to them:
  reviews of this app and of competitors (three-star reviews say both what they
  wanted and what disappointed them), forum threads, support mail, sales calls.
  Copy their phrases into a list with the source. A phrase readers use themselves
  beats a phrase the team invented. `/sprid:research` builds this list.
- **Read the alternatives.** Fetch what competitors and substitutes say in their
  headlines. Note the frame each one takes. Repeating the frame everyone uses makes
  the app one of many; a frame nobody uses is either an opening or a sign the
  readers do not care. Check which with the readers' own words.
- **Ask the people who already chose it.** If the app has users, ask a few: what
  were you doing before, what made you look, what almost stopped you, what would
  you tell a friend it does? Their answers usually contain the headline.

## Questions that find the frame

- **What does the reader already know?** Someone who does not know they have the
  problem needs the problem named first. Someone comparing tools needs the
  difference. Someone who knows the app needs a reason to act now. Open where the
  reader is (after Eugene Schwartz's stages of awareness).
- **Before, after, and how.** What is the reader's situation now, in their words?
  What is it after? What gets them from one to the other? The headline usually
  states the after; the line under it the how.
- **Compared with what?** What would the reader use if this app did not exist: a
  competitor, a spreadsheet, a person, doing nothing? The app's value is its
  difference from that alternative, said in the reader's terms (after April
  Dunford's positioning method).
- **What pushes, what pulls, what holds them back?** The frustration with how
  things are now, the attraction of the new way, the worry about switching, and the
  habit of the old way all act at once. Copy that only sells the new way leaves the
  worry and the habit unanswered (after the jobs-to-be-done forces of progress).
- **Can they picture it?** A concrete scene or a true number ("dinner from what's
  in your fridge") is remembered; an abstraction ("smarter meal planning") is not.

## Try several frames, then choose

Write the same headline in five frames before picking one: the result, the pain,
the comparison, who it is for, how it works. Read each against the brief and the
competitor pages. Pick on evidence, and keep the runners-up.

The runners-up can be tested cheaply before the page changes. Post two or three
frames as social posts or small ads to the same audience and see which one people
react to, save or click. The page then gets the frame the readers chose.
