# Why a viewer keeps going

Use while writing and reviewing a carousel, a reel, a video ad or the opening
of a caption. These are priors about how a person in a feed decides to stay.
Reason with them about the post in front of you; do not recite them to the
user or turn them into a score, a template or a checklist. The account's voice,
accepted examples and measured results outrank every line here, and a post that
breaks several of them and still holds people is the better post.

## Reuse the format, write the idea fresh

A repeatable format is fine. An account template, an ad
[archetype](../skills/ads/archetypes.md) or a shot list rebuilt from a
[teardown](../skills/scout/teardown.md) is how a series gets made every week.
What should not be reused is the thinking inside it: work out the opening, the
order of the lines and the payoff from this viewer and this material each time.

The script has slipped into formula when:

- the structure was chosen before the idea was clear;
- it would still read fine with another product or subject swapped in;
- the hook is an earlier hook with new nouns.

Then go back to the brief in [content value](../skills/post/references/content-value.md)
and start from what this viewer gets.

## Write to one person

Picture one viewer from the research and write as if sending the post to them
directly. Greetings, a reason for making the post and explanations of what they
already know fall away on their own.

Put the detail in the thing, not in the audience. A named amount, place, date
or object makes a line concrete. Opening on who the viewer is (“if you run a
café…”) narrows who keeps reading. A call-out can still be right, for example
in an ad where the reader has to recognise themselves: write it as one of the
alternatives and compare.

## Every line has to earn the next

A feed is read one line at a time and the viewer can leave at each one. In a
carousel every swipe is a deliberate decision. So the review question is local:
does slide 1 make slide 2 necessary, does line 2 make line 3 necessary? A strong
payoff behind a flat middle is never seen.

Give each line just enough to make the next one needed. Read only the opening
and stop. If nothing is left unanswered, the post has given the viewer no reason
to continue.

## Keep a question open

The opening is the first question the viewer holds, and the post runs on a chain
of them. Raise a question before answering it: an answer nobody was waiting for
is just information. Raise the next question before the current one is settled,
because a viewer who feels finished leaves. When the last promised answer lands,
stop, as in [build toward the payoff](../skills/reels/guides/first-seconds.md#build-toward-the-payoff).

The viewer has to be the one wondering. A person on screen asking a question or
looking surprised gives the viewer someone else's curiosity to watch. Turn it
into something the viewer wants to know.

Pay off what was set up. Revealing a serious setup as a joke or a bait teaches
the viewer that this account's openings do not pay, and that costs the next
post too.

## Price the time

The viewer weighs how useful the post looks against how long it will take.
Make the value clear early and the cost feel small. Stating the cost can help
(“three settings, thirty seconds”), and a carousel shows its own length. A
longer post is fine when every part pays.

Deliver something early, not only a promise. In a story or a test, get to the
event: the setup is not the content. Keep each card readable while doing it,
as in [match timing to the content](../skills/reels/guides/first-seconds.md#match-timing-to-the-content).

## Specific, and true

In each line, look for the broad summary that a real number, name, date or
object could replace: “every Sunday night” for “regularly”, the actual screen
for “the app”. A specific object gives the viewer something to picture. A
category (“the best tools for X”) gives them nothing to wonder about.

The detail comes from the material: the product, the founder's
[own material](founder-material.md), sourced research. Never invent one to sound
specific; the [writing gate's proof rules](writing-gate.md#3-proof) apply. If the
real detail is missing, ask for it or mark it `[NEED: detail]`.

## A new idea, said simply

If the idea cannot be said in about ten words, it is probably not clear yet, or
not new. Fix that before the wording. Then simplify the sentences, not the idea:
read each line as someone with no context would, and replace shorthand with the
thing they can picture. Keep a precise term the audience uses; explain any
other in the same line.

## One centre, cut the rest

Name the one thing the post is about: what is learned, shown, tested or waited
for. Cut what does not serve it, including lines that are interesting but beside
the point, a preamble that does not feed it and silent stretches in a video.
Prefer short sentences: the format rewards many ideas in few words.

## Answer the doubt where it lands

Every post gives the viewer a reason to leave, usually one of a few: this is an
ad; I could not do this; it only works with money, an audience or a team; I have
seen this before; it will take too long; it does not apply to me. Find the one
this post raises and answer it in a short line at the moment it appears, not at
the end. In a product post, showing the useful result before the product's name
can answer the first.

## For video: beats and frames

How long one frame is held matters more than whether it shows a face or a
screen. A script with more distinct beats gives the edit more places to cut.
Cuts refresh attention, but the open question is what holds it: fast editing
does not rescue a post that has none.

## Test them on this account

Each of these is a hypothesis for this audience. Where Sprid reports them,
`skipRate` reads the opening and `watchedShare` how much of the rest was seen;
see [diagnose with available evidence](../skills/reels/guides/first-seconds.md#diagnose-with-available-evidence).
Change one at a time against comparable posts and keep the account's own results
above anything written here.

Several of these priors follow Remy Gaskell's
[content principles](https://github.com/aiwithremy/content-principles).
