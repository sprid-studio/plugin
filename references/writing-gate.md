# The writing gate

Run this on any copy a stranger will read before deciding about the app: a homepage,
a landing page, a search page, a store listing, an ad. Readers are suspicious of
machine-written text, and they decide in seconds. The copy has to read as if a person
who knows the product wrote it. Fix every hit, then run the gate again.

## 1. Mechanical checks

Search the draft for these and read each hit. A real guarantee can survive; a rhythm
device cannot.

- **Dashes.** Em and en dashes. Use a comma, a colon, a full stop or a hyphen with spaces.
- **Contrast reveals.** "Not X, but Y", "X, not Y", "It's not about X", "rather than",
  "instead of", "more than just". Say the positive thing. Keep a negation only when it
  states what the product will or will not do: "No account needed", "Nothing posts
  without you".
- **Colon and question reveals.** "The result?", "Here's the thing:", "The catch:".
- **Triplets.** Rhetorical lists of three ("faster, simpler, smarter"). Lists of real
  things are fine.
- **Balanced pairs.** Two clauses with the same shape ("Your data. Your rules.").
  Break one, or merge them into one sentence that says what happens.
- **Aphoristic closers.** A last line with a turn that could be printed on a mug.
  Replace it with the literal fact it was dressing up.
- **Filler words.** seamless, robust, leverage, unlock, empower, supercharge,
  effortless, streamline, journey, landscape, delve, game-changing, "in today's".
- **Long sentences.** Re-read every sentence over 20 words. Split it or cut it.
- **Exclamation marks** in body copy.

## 2. Meaning tests

- **Swap test.** Could this line sit unchanged on a competitor's page, or on any
  app's page? Then it says nothing. Name the thing only this product does, or mark
  it `[NEED: differentiator]` and ask.
- **"Now you can" test.** Put "Now you can" in front of the headline. The result must
  be true and something the reader wants.
- **Picture test.** Can the reader picture it? "Dinner from what's in your fridge"
  passes; "smarter meal planning" does not.
- **Antecedent test.** Every "it", "this", "the work" points at something named in
  the same block.
- **Reader as subject.** Write what the reader gets or does. "You see which post
  brought the signups" beats "The app tracks attribution".
- **Their words.** Prefer phrases from the app's research and reviews over the
  team's internal vocabulary. A developer term is fine when the reader is a developer.

## 3. Proof

- Never invent a number, a testimonial, a rating, a customer count, a logo or a
  result. Where proof would help and none exists, write `[NEED: proof]` and ask.
- A rewrite that adds a fact, a name or a number the source did not contain is an
  error, even if it sounds plausible.
- Examples and illustrations use clearly fictional apps or the customer's own
  real screens. Structured data (ratings, reviews, prices) matches what is publicly
  true on the day it ships.
- A competitor fact carries its date and where it was read: "not listed on their
  pricing page (3 Oct 2026)". A feature you did not see may still exist.

## 4. Read it like a stranger

1. Read only the headings, top to bottom. They should tell the whole story on their
   own, each from a different angle. If two headings say the same thing, one goes.
2. Read one section aloud. Does it sound like something you would say to a person,
   or something you would print?
3. Count the small text. If the page is mostly paragraphs, cut each paragraph to one
   or two lines and move the details into links, lists or the next page.
