# Ad persuasion: desire, awareness and the swipe

Use while planning, drafting or iterating paid creatives alongside
[why a viewer keeps going](viewer-attention.md) and
[finding the frame](copy-framing.md). These are reasoning priors about why a
cold or warm buyer stops, swipes and believes a paid ad. Treat every line as a
testable hypothesis, never as a score, template or checklist. The account's
voice, accepted examples and measured paid outcomes outrank everything here.

## Channel one desire

An ad does not create desire; it channels a desire the buyer already brings
into the feed. Pick the single strongest desire by how strongly people want it,
how often it comes back and how many buyers share it, then let every card or
line serve that one desire.

A carousel that mixes unrelated benefits (cost, sun, checklists, sharing) asks
the viewer to care about four jobs at once and weakens the one reason they
would pay now. Cut any card that does not deepen the chosen desire.

## Match how aware the buyer is

Open where the buyer actually stands (after Eugene Schwartz's five stages of
awareness), not where the team wishes they stood:

- **Unaware:** no product, no price and no claim in the opening. Call out a
  recognisable moment, phrase or tension from their world.
- **Problem-aware:** name the concrete cost or friction of the problem first,
  then bring in the product as the way to handle it.
- **Solution-aware:** they already want the result; show that it is possible
  and the specific mechanism by which this product delivers it.
- **Product-aware:** they know the product exists; intensify concrete proof and
  remove the remaining doubt before asking for the action.
- **Most-aware:** show the product and the exact deal plainly. Extra setup
  slows down someone who is already ready to act.

## Match how jaded the market is

A promise that worked in a fresh category gets ignored once buyers have heard
it ten times (market sophistication):

- **Early category (stages 1–2):** state the core claim simply and prove it, or
  make the concrete outcome sharper.
- **Crowded claims (stage 3):** introduce a **mechanism** - the concrete way
  the product produces the result (for example reading a source document and
  linking every point to its page), not a louder adjective.
- **Copied mechanisms (stage 4):** show why this mechanism is faster, simpler
  or easier to verify.
- **Ad-blind audience (stage 5):** stop arguing like an ad. Shift to
  **identification** (who the buyer becomes) or **camouflage** (below).

## Earn the swipe, and do not gatekeep on a broad CPM

The opening's only job is to make the viewer take the next step: swipe card 2,
watch the next seconds or tap through. Read card 1 on its own and stop. If card
1 already prints the translation, answer or verdict, there is no reason to
swipe. Hold the breakdown, plain-language translation or cost figure for card 2
(unless the creative is a deliberate single-frame offer for most-aware traffic).

Paid feeds bill per impression (CPM), not per swipe or click. At low budget and
early conversion volume, calling out an overly narrow sub-segment on card 1
(one build decade, one niche profession) makes the account pay CPM across broad
buyers while self-excluding people who would still buy.

- **Filter on the buying moment, not a narrow attribute.** Open on the moment
  every real buyer shares (preparing a bid, viewing this weekend, holding a
  long technical document). That screens out non-buyers while keeping all
  active buyers in the room.
- **Put the specific detail in the example, not the door.** Keep real figures,
  dates and concrete cases inside the proof (card 2 onward) where every buyer
  asks "what does mine look like?". Split into sub-segment hooks only once
  conversion volume and budget support parallel creative tracks.

## Build belief before the price

One specific claim the buyer fully believes beats ten promises they half
believe. Use **gradualization** when asking cold traffic to pay: guide the
viewer through two or three small, verifiable steps they already agree with
(a familiar surface -> the hidden catch -> the real cost anchor) before stating
the offer and price.

Pair it with **intensification**: show the concrete consequence or the product
in action so the offer price lands against a real scale rather than cold air.
Label sample cases plainly as examples ([ad images](ad-images.md#each-card)),
and avoid repeating the exact same figure in both the headline and the lifted
card.

## Borrow trust with camouflage

Feed readers tune out polished ad frames on sight. **Camouflage** borrows the
trust of informational posts: an unpolished photograph, a real category quote
or an editorial note with no device mockup or brand chrome on card 1. Treat it
as a deliberate, named departure from the product's standard mockup set
([ad images](ad-images.md#before-designing)), and bridge cleanly into the
product and exact offer on the next card or landing page.

## Diagnose creative separately from post-click

When an ad produces spend without purchases, separate where the chain broke
before rewriting hooks ([campaign promise check](campaign-promise-check.md)):

- **Low CTR or weak opening hold:** card 1 failed to catch the buying moment,
  closed its own loop too early, or narrowed out real buyers on a broad CPM.
- **Healthy opening, weak carousel depth or ThruPlay:** card 2 did not pay off
  the question card 1 opened, or competing desires diluted the middle.
- **Healthy clicks and landing starts, zero purchases:** do not assume another
  hook will fix it. Check offer and price match, checkout friction, tracking
  and whether the creative attracted curiosity clicks instead of buyers in the
  moment.

Several of these concepts draw on Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*
(mass desire, awareness, market sophistication, gradualization, intensification,
mechanization and camouflage), adapted as testable hypotheses for paid feeds.
