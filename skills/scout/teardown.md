# Reading a sheet: how to take a post apart

A sheet is a few frames of somebody's post, in time order. It is enough to
describe a format precisely and rebuild it, and it is not enough to claim you
know what the video does. This page is the order to read it in and the words to
use, so that two teardowns of two posts can be compared.

The output of a teardown is never "this was good". It is a **rebuild spec**: what
the format is made of, what it costs to make, and what the user would have to
change to make one of their own.

## Read it in this order

**1. Frame 0, alone, before you look at anything else.** It is the cover the feed
shows before a single millisecond plays. Ask only: what does it say, is the text
finished and legible, and does it promise something. A cover that is mid-fade,
half-lit, or carrying a sentence nobody can finish in a scroll is the single most
expensive mistake in the format, and it is visible here and nowhere else.

**2. The deltas.** Walk the frames in order and name what changed between each
pair: the subject moved, the camera moved, the text changed, the scene cut.
Change is the format's engine. If two frames seconds apart are nearly identical,
the post is holding a viewer with continuity rather than with cuts, and that is a
different kind of format from one that has cut three times by second five.

**3. The constants.** What is in every frame. That is the format's identity, and
it is what the user would have to reproduce.

## The six things to name

Name them plainly, in the user's language, as observations rather than praise.

- **Subject.** What is physically in the frame. Money on a table, a phone
  screen, a face, a product, a room.
- **Setting and production.** Where it was shot and what it cost: a phone on a
  tripod over a kitchen table, a lit studio, a screen recording, stock footage.
  Say what equipment the user would need. This is usually the finding that
  decides whether a format is available to them at all.
- **Who is on screen.** Nobody, hands only, one person talking, several people.
  Hands-only and faceless formats are repeatable by anyone; a format carried by
  one person's presence is not transferable, however well it performs.
- **The action.** What is being done, in verbs. Counting, unpacking, pointing,
  writing, scrolling, walking. A format is usually one action repeated, and
  naming it is most of the rebuild.
- **Text treatment.** How many text elements, where they sit, whether they hold
  or change, and whether they are burned into the picture or captions. Note
  whether a title card persists past the opening - a card that holds for five
  seconds is doing the work a cut would otherwise do.
- **Palette and mood.** The colours and what they signal. Say it concretely
  ("pastel pinks on white, domestic, calm") rather than as a vibe word.

## Then two judgements, marked as judgements

**What the format promises.** In one sentence, from the cover and the first
seconds only: what does a viewer think they are about to get. This is the thing
the user's own version has to promise too, in their own subject.

**Why it is repeatable, or why it is not.** A format that needs one person's
face, a location, or an expensive object is a format the user cannot run weekly.
One that needs a table, a phone and an action can be run fifty times. Say which,
and say what the weekly cost of running it would be.

## Across posts: the wall

One sheet reads a post down time. A **wall** reads a niche across posts: the same
second from a dozen of them, four to a row. Frame 0 is the default, because that
is the cover the feed shows, and a wall of covers is the fastest way to see what
a category has already agreed on.

```
sprid-scout-quick wall <post-url> <post-url> … [--at-second 0]
```

Read a wall for three things, in this order:

1. **What repeats.** A habit shared by most of the wall is the category's
   convention. Matching it is table stakes; the user is not going to win on it.
2. **What appears once.** The cell that looks unlike the others is either a
   mistake or the opening. Say which you think it is and why.
3. **What is missing entirely.** A shape absent from a whole wall is the most
   interesting cell on it, and the hardest to see, because nothing is there to
   look at. Name it explicitly or you will not notice it.

Then a second wall at two or three seconds answers the question the first one
raises: of the covers that promised something, which ones deliver a change by
the time the skip window closes.

A wall of twelve is usually enough to call a convention; a wall of four is an
anecdote with a nice layout.

## Budget the frames

Frames are cheap to take and expensive to read: past roughly fifty in one
context, a model starts losing the early ones. Spend them deliberately - five
offsets on three posts and a twelve-cell wall is forty-seven frames and a
complete picture of a niche. Sampling one post at twenty offsets is almost
always the worse buy.

## For an ad, three more things to name

An ad is a post with money behind it, so everything above still applies. What it
adds is that somebody chose a **reason** it should work, and that reason is what
transfers - not the picture.

- **The angle.** The perspective the story is told from, independent of the
  visual: problem-led, benefit-led, social-proof-led, comparison, identity. Two
  ads can share a shape and be different angles, and the angle is what you test
  against, not the art.
- **The offer framing.** How the deal is stated: a percentage, a money amount, a
  free trial, "pay once". The framing changes performance more than the wording
  around it.
- **The call to action, in its exact words.** Quote it. It is the shortest line
  in the ad and the most copied.

And the one number the ad library gives: **days live**, which means somebody
keeps paying, not that it is efficient. A long-running ad plus a shape that
repeats across several advertisers is the strongest evidence available here.
Pair the wall with it: twelve competitor ads at frame 0, sorted by days live,
shows what the category's survivors open with.

## What a sheet cannot tell you

Say these out loud rather than guessing past them. Getting this wrong is how a
teardown turns into a confident description of a video nobody watched.

- **No sound.** Music, voice-over, speech and their pacing are absent entirely.
  A format that is carried by a spoken script looks identical on a sheet to one
  carried by text.
- **Nothing between the samples.** Five frames of a four-minute post leave
  minutes unseen. Anything you say about the parts you did not sample is a
  guess, and should be labelled one or checked by sampling again.
- **No cuts between samples.** Fixed offsets cannot see a cut that happened
  between two of them, so "no cuts in the first eight seconds" is only true of
  the seconds you sampled. If cut rhythm is the question, sample every half
  second across the opening rather than guessing.
- **No retention.** The sheet says nothing about where people left. The post's
  own numbers say how far it travelled, not where it lost anyone.
- **Views are not quality.** A post with millions of views proves distribution
  happened, not that the format caused it. Look at several posts from the same
  account before you attribute anything to the shape.

When a reading depends on one of these, sample again at the offsets that would
settle it, rather than reasoning around the gap. Frames are cheap.

## Turn it into a rebuild

Close every teardown with the thing the user can act on:

1. **The shot list** - three to six lines, in order, each naming a frame and what
   is in it.
2. **The swap** - what replaces the subject for this product. The action usually
   survives the swap; the object rarely does.
3. **The cost** - what they need to shoot it once, and what it costs to shoot it
   again next week.

And the rule that governs all of it: **the frames are evidence, not material.**
They exist so the structure can be described and rebuilt in the user's own
product, voice and images. They are never republished, never placed in a draft,
and never handed back as something to post.

## Where this method comes from

Written from what the frames can actually show, plus two things already measured
here: the opening seconds decide whether a video is watched at all, and a cover
frame that is not finished at t=0 is the most expensive mistake available. The
ad-side vocabulary (angle, offer framing, fatigue) is the standard one used in
creative testing, and it is here because it is what makes two teardowns
comparable, not because it is ours.

It is deliberately not a film-grammar rubric. Framing, camera movement, eye-trace
and cut type are worth naming when the user is directing a shoot; for deciding
what to post next week, what the subject is, what is being done and what the
first frame promises carry almost all of the signal.
