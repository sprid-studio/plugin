# Understanding the user's product first

Some people here want to promote something: an app, a product, a brand. Learn to recognise that pattern, because it changes what good help looks like. You cannot find the right accounts or write the right keywords until you understand what the product is, and chasing the wrong niche wastes an hour of browser time.

That pattern is a moment to read rather than a habit to force on every conversation. Plenty of users are decisive: when someone names a clear niche outright they have already told you where to look, and interrogating them for a link and a positioning statement only gets in the way. Reach for this when someone has a product they want grown. When you do, the method is a chain: research the product the way a marketer would, hold a structured understanding of it, then derive the niche and the keywords from that understanding.

## Research it like a marketer

Understand the product deeply enough that you could market it yourself. Public research gets you most of the way there before you ask the user anything. What a marketer would reach for: the product's own site and store listing, where it says what it thinks it is; its screenshots and visual identity, the colours and tone it carries; its competitors, who else is in the space and how they position; and its reviews, where real users say in their own words what they praise and complain about, which is often where the sharpest emotional hooks hide.

Pull the thread the particular product rewards. A beautiful consumer app repays a hard look at its visuals; a dry utility repays its reviews.

## Hold a structured understanding

From that research, build and keep a clear picture covering:

- **Name and tagline** as the product states them.
- **Category**: what kind of product this is.
- **What it does**: the core function, in one honest sentence.
- **Features**: the concrete capabilities, not the marketing adjectives.
- **Target audience**: who it is for, specifically.
- **Value propositions**: the reasons someone chooses it.
- **Monetization**: how it makes money, free versus paid, price points.
- **Visual identity**: colours, tone, the look the content should match.
- **Emotional angles**: the feelings it sells on (confidence, relief, status, belonging), drawn especially from reviews.
- **Competitors**: who else, and how they differ.
- **Review sentiment**: what real users love and hate.

## Ask the owner only what research cannot resolve

Public research answers most of this. Do not manufacture a questionnaire. Ask the owner only when the answer is both unavailable publicly **and** would change the marketing. That short list is usually:

- **Private context**: channels they have already tried and how those went, what has worked and what has flopped.
- **Intended positioning**: where they want to take the brand, if it differs from where it is now.

Never invent questions to look thorough. A question you could have answered by reading the site erodes trust. When the owner does answer, their answers are authoritative and override anything you inferred.

## From understanding to niche to keywords

**Derive the niche from the understanding.** Ask where slideshow content about this product's world already exists in volume. The product's exact category is usually too narrow to carry much slideshow volume of its own. What you want is the widest world its audience already scrolls, the broad niche the product sits inside, with its exact category as one corner within it. A niche with more slideshow volume gives the run more accounts to draw from, more overperformers to surface, and more replicable formats to study.

**Then spread down into sub-niches and concrete searches.** From the umbrella, branch into the specific corners the product actually touches, and from those into the exact short phrases real people type. Vary the intent behind each search, because the same niche is searched by people wanting to fix a problem, buy a solution, follow a routine, or see proof it works. A good keyword list covers that whole spread: a few broad umbrella terms up top, then many specific, human, intent-varied searches beneath them.

Whatever the product, the move is the same: find the abundant umbrella its audience already scrolls, then spread down into the corners it actually touches. discover.md covers how keyword order, count, and filters shape the run from there.
